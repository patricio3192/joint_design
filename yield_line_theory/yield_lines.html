Yield-line analysis of steel plates

A hand-calculation guide written for the plate checks of cantilever_anchor_V2. Units: N, mm, MPa. With Fy in MPa and t in mm, the plastic moment per unit length mp comes out in N·mm/mm, which is simply N. Multiply it by a length in mm to get N·mm.

Every formula and number on this page was re-derived and checked by yl_verify.m (section 15). Code clauses were read in the PDFs of your Bibliography4structures folder; page numbers are given in section 14.

0. Start here: where V2 stands

V2 does not use yield lines yet. Section 3 of the V2 report checks the 12 mm embed plate with strips. A strip is a beam cut out of the plate that carries a load alone, with the rest of the plate ignored. In plastic theory that is the lower-bound (static) method, which is always safe. Yield lines are the upper-bound (kinematic) method: you guess how the plate folds and get a load that is at or above the true one. This page teaches both, because you need both to judge a plate. Section 11 puts each V2 check in its place.

How to read this page: sections 1–4 are the theory you need, sections 5–10 are worked examples from simple to code formulas, and every example is done with numbers you can follow on a hand calculator. The calculators in the grey boxes only exist to check your hand work.

1. The idea in one picture

Load a steel plate until it collapses. First it bends elastically. Then the most stressed points yield through the whole thickness, and the yielding spreads along lines. When enough lines have formed, the plate becomes a mechanism: flat, rigid pieces of plate turning about their supports and hinged to each other along the yield lines, like a sheet of paper folded along creases (Bruneau suggests trying exactly that).

The assumptions, and why they are reasonable for steel plates:

  • Rigid-plastic material. Elastic deflections are ignored; all rotation happens at the yield lines. At collapse the plastic rotations are much larger than the elastic ones (DG 16 §2.2).
  • Along every yield line the bending moment is the plastic moment mp per unit length. Steel has the same mp in both directions of bending, so positive and negative lines cost the same (Bruneau eq. 4.40).
  • Small displacements. Membrane (stretching) action and strain hardening are ignored. Both make real plates stronger at large deflection (section 12).
  • Bending only. Shear, punching and weld/bolt strength are separate checks.

2. Plastic moment of a plate, per unit length

Cut a strip of plate 1 mm wide and t thick. Elastic bending stops when the extreme fibre reaches Fy. Fully plastic bending has Fy over the whole half-thickness, in tension on one side and compression on the other.

mp = Fy t² / 4    [N·mm/mm = N]

The ratio mp/my = Z/S = 1.5 for a rectangle. AISC 360-16 F11.1 (rectangular bars) gives Mn = Mp = FyZ ≤ 1.6 FyS. Because 1.5 < 1.6, the full plastic moment is allowed, with φb = 0.90 (F1). AISC J10.10 sends transverse loads on plates to J4.5 (flexure) and, in its User Note, to yield-line theory.

Going deeper: why Fy t²/4 and not more

Along a long straight yield line the plate cannot stretch along the line, so the steel there yields in plane strain. With the von Mises criterion the plane-strain yield stress is 2/√3 = 1.155 Fy, so a real steel hinge line carries up to 15 % more than Fy t²/4. All design formulas (DG 16, AISC 358, AISC 360 Chapter K) use Fy t²/4 and so does this page. A yield-line result is then exact for a slightly weaker idealized plate (Tresca). That margin is real, but it does not make a wrong mechanism safe.

3. Upper and lower bounds: the three theorems

A collapse load is exact when three conditions hold at once (Bruneau §4.3):

  1. Equilibrium: the internal moments balance the loads everywhere.
  2. Yield: nowhere does the moment exceed mp.
  3. Mechanism: enough yield lines form to let the plate move.

Finding a solution that meets all three is hard. The two practical methods each give up one condition, and the theorems tell you which side of the truth you land on:

MethodYou satisfyYou skipResultSafe?
Static (lower bound): strips, moment fieldsequilibrium + yieldmechanismload ≤ true collapse loadalways
Kinematic (upper bound): yield linesmechanism + workyield everywhereload ≥ true collapse loadonly if the mechanism is the right one
The three theorems (Bruneau §4.3.1–4.3.3) Upper bound: a collapse load computed from an assumed mechanism is greater than or equal to the true collapse load.
Lower bound: a load computed from a moment distribution that is in equilibrium and nowhere exceeds mp is less than or equal to the true collapse load.
Uniqueness: the true collapse load is the one where the upper and lower bounds meet.

What this means for design:

  • A passing lower bound is enough. That is what V2 does with its strips.
  • An upper bound on its own can be unsafe. You must find the least upper bound: try several patterns, optimise their free dimensions, and take the smallest result (DG 16 §2.2: “one must find the least upper bound”). Or use a code formula whose pattern was confirmed by tests (sections 9 and 10).
  • Both together bracket the truth. If the lower bound fails but the upper bound passes, you know where to look for reserve. Charts 2 and 3 show such a bracket.

4. The work equation: a recipe you can follow by hand

Give the mechanism a small virtual displacement. The work done by the loads must equal the work dissipated in the yield lines:

WE = WI

External work WE, from the loads moving with the plate:

  • point load P moving δ:  WE = P δ
  • uniform pressure w:  WE = w × (volume under the deflected plate)
  • line load q (N/mm):  WE = q × (area under the deflection along the line)

Internal work WI, from each yield line of length ℓ that opens by a relative rotation θn:

WI = Σ mp θn ℓ Bruneau §4.6.1

The projection shortcut. Working out θn for sloping lines is tedious. Instead, sum region by region: each rigid region turns by θ about its own axis, and its work is mp θ times the length of its yield lines projected onto that axis. This is Bruneau eq. 4.44, WI = mp(ℓxθx + ℓyθy), applied one region at a time:

Each yield line between two moving regions is then counted twice, once from each side, which is right: it opens by the sum of the two rotations.

The recipe

  1. Draw the plate: supports (clamped, simply supported, point supports such as bolts), free edges, loads.
  2. Choose a yield-line pattern that obeys the rules below. Leave its free dimensions as unknowns (x, e, …).
  3. Set the largest deflection to δ = 1 (any value works; it cancels).
  4. For each region find its axis and its rotation θ = δ / (distance from the axis to the point that moves δ).
  5. WI = mp Σ θ × (projected length of the region’s yield lines). Add negative lines along clamped or continuous edges; none along simply supported or free edges.
  6. WE from the loads.
  7. Solve WE = WI for the load. If the pattern has a free dimension, find the minimum: set the derivative to zero, or tabulate a few values on the calculator.
  8. Then do the checks yield lines leave out: shear, welds, bolts, stiffness (section 12).

Rules for a valid pattern

  1. Yield lines are straight. They end at a plate edge or at another yield line. (A curved line is the limit of many short straight ones: section 7.)
  2. Each region turns about an axis. Axes lie along supported edges and pass over point supports such as bolts or columns.
  3. The yield line between two regions passes through the intersection of their axes (or is parallel to both if they are parallel).
  4. A moving region may touch the plate that does not move only along its own axis. Anywhere else the deflection would jump and the plate would tear.
  5. A clamped or continuous edge that is crossed by the mechanism carries a negative yield line. A simply supported or free edge carries none.
Rule 4 is the trap. Patterns that look natural on paper often break it. The work equation still gives a number, and that number is too low: it is not a bound of anything. Figure 8 in section 8 shows a real example: without rule 4 you would get almost zero strength for a narrow load. yl_work.m checks rule 4 for every pattern on this page.

5. Strips (one-way bending): yield lines you already know

A strip of width b and span L is a beam. Its yield lines are the plastic hinges of beam theory. This is the case V2 uses.

5.1 Simply supported strip, uniform load (total F)

  1. One positive line across the strip at mid-span, length b. Each half turns θ = δ/(L/2) = 2δ/L about its support.
  2. WI = mp × b × (opening 2θ) = mp b × 4δ/L.
  3. The deflected shape is a triangle; the average deflection is δ/2, so WE = F δ/2.
  4. F δ/2 = 4 mp b δ/L ⇒ F = 8 mp b / L. In beam terms: Mp = F L/8 with Mp = mp b. Same thing.

5.2 The other three strip cases

5.3 The V2 numbers (report section 3b)

Over the 40 mm gap between the two platinas, the strip of plate behind the flange footprint (b = tf + 2w = 9.8 + 16 = 25.8 mm) carries the flange share sideways to the platinas.

  1. Load on the strip: F = Tflange × gap/bf = 82.73 × 40/120 = 27.58 kN.
  2. mp = 8 928 N (12 mm A36). Simply supported: Fn = 8 × 8 928 × 25.8 / 40 = 46 067 N; φFn = 0.9 × 46.07 = 41.46 kN.
  3. D/C = 27.58 / 41.46 = 0.67. With clamped ends (16 instead of 8): φFn = 82.92 kN, D/C = 0.33. These are the report values.

So report step 3b is a yield-line calculation: one-dimensional, and exact for the strip on its own. For the whole plate it is a lower bound (section 11).

6. Plates under pressure: where two-way patterns start

6.1 Simply supported square plate, uniform pressure w (Figure 1)

  1. Each triangle: θ = 2δ/a. Its two half-diagonals project onto its edge with total length a.
  2. WI = 4 triangles × mp (2δ/a) × a = 8 mp δ. No lines along the edges: they are simply supported.
  3. The deflected plate is a pyramid of height δ: volume = a²δ/3, so WE = w a² δ/3.
  4. w a²δ/3 = 8 mpδ ⇒ w = 24 mp / a² Bruneau eq. 4.48. With clamped edges, negative lines along all four edges double WI: w = 48 mp/a².
a = 300 mm, t = 12 mm A36:   w = 24 × 8 928 / 300² = 2.38 MPa (2 380 kN/m²)   upper bound

A lower bound for the same plate. Take the moment field mx = mp(1 − 4x²/a²), my = mp(1 − 4y²/a²), mxy = 0, with x, y measured from the centre. It is zero on the edges (simply supported), never exceeds mp, and satisfies the plate equilibrium equation

∂²mx/∂x² + 2 ∂²mxy/∂x∂y + ∂²my/∂y² = −w  ⇒  −8mp/a² + 0 − 8mp/a² = −w  ⇒  w = 16 mp/a² = 1.59 MPa

So the true collapse pressure is between 1.59 and 2.38 MPa. Neither number is the answer; together they are an honest statement of what you know.

Going deeper: is 24 mp/a² exact? (corner levers)

Bruneau (Fig. 4.27, pp. 227–228) reports that letting the corners lever up, with a short negative line across each corner, lowers the result to about 22 mp/a², about 9 % less. That figure comes from reinforced-concrete slab studies (Park & Gamble), where the corners are usually weak in hogging or free to lift.

With equal hogging and sagging strength and the corners held down, no hinge-line pattern can go below 24. The Prager field mxy = −4mpxy/a² (with the same mx, my) carries exactly 24 mp/a² and meets the square (Johansen) yield criterion of slab theory. But at the corners it has principal moments +mp and −mp, which a steel plate cannot take: yl_verify.m finds 2.00 × the Tresca limit and 1.73 × the von Mises limit. So for a steel plate 24 is not proven exact. The safe statement stays the bracket 16 to 24.

The lesson: even the textbook pattern of the simplest plate is not guaranteed to be the least upper bound. That is why codes use patterns confirmed by tests.

6.2 Simply supported rectangle a × b (a the short side): a pattern with a free dimension

  1. Trapezoids (2): θ = 2δ/a; their lines project onto the long edge with length b. Triangles (2): θ = δ/x; projection a.
  2. WI = mpδ (2 × b × 2/a + 2 × a/x) = mpδ (4b/a + 2a/x).
  3. Volume = a(3b − 2x)δ/6 (a prism in the middle plus two pyramids), so WE = w a(3b − 2x)δ/6.
  4. Equate: w(x) = 12 mp (2bx + a²) / [a² x (3b − 2x)] Bruneau eq. 4.53
  5. Minimise, dw/dx = 0: x = (a/2b)(−a + √(a² + 3b²)) eq. 4.54. Putting it back gives a closed form you can use directly:
wu = 24 mp / [ a² ( √(3 + (a/b)²) − a/b )² ]

For a square (a/b = 1) the bracket is (2 − 1)² = 1 and wu = 24 mp/a² as in 6.1. A lower bound valid for steel is again the two-way field: wl = 8 mp(1/a² + 1/b²).

a = 200, b = 400, t = 12 A36 (mp = 8 928):   a/b = 0.5; √3.25 = 1.8028; (1.8028 − 0.5)² = 1.6972
wu = 24 × 8 928 / (200² × 1.6972) = 3.16 MPa at x = 0.25 (−200 + √520 000) = 130.3 mm
wl = 8 × 8 928 (1/200² + 1/400²) = 2.23 MPa;   one-way strip only: 8 × 8 928/200² = 1.79 MPa

Chart 2. Each value of x is a different mechanism and each gives an upper bound. The least of them, 3.16 MPa at x = 130 mm, is the best upper bound this pattern can give. The true collapse pressure lies in the shaded band, above the best lower bound.

7. Point loads: fans, and why the circle wins

Push a plate at one point, far from its edges. The plate folds into a cone of triangles around the load. Take n triangles forming a regular polygon. Each triangle turns about its outer edge, which is a negative line (the plate outside does not move). Its positive lines run to the load.

  1. One triangle: edge length ℓ, distance h from the load. θ = δ/h. Its positive lines project onto its edge with length ℓ, and the negative edge adds another ℓ.
  2. WI = Σ 2 mp (δ/h) ℓ. For a regular n-gon, ℓ/h = 2 tan(π/n), so WI = 2 × 2n tan(π/n) mp δ. WE = Pδ.
  3. P = 4n tan(π/n) mp. It falls as n grows and tends to 4π mp: the circle is the least upper bound of this family.

Chart 4. Positive part only, 2n tan(π/n), against the number of triangles. It falls quickly to 2π. The full fan with the negative boundary is twice these values.

continuous plate: P = 4π mp
simply supported circular plate, load at the centre: P = 2π mp

Remarkably, the result does not depend on the size of the fan (as long as it fits in the plate). For a load spread over a small circle of radius c in a clamped circular plate of radius R, the cone mechanism gives P = 4π mp / (1 − 2c/3R).

12 mm A36: 4π × 8 928 = 112.2 kN; φ = 0.9: 101.0 kN
Check shear too. A point load really acts on a small patch, and yield lines ignore shear. The plate can punch through first: φVn = 1.0 × 0.6 Fy t × (perimeter of the loaded patch), AISC J4.2(a), with the perimeter taken around the weld toes. For a 12 mm A36 plate that is 1.79 kN per mm of perimeter: a 63 mm perimeter already gives about the same 112 kN.

The fan is the reason the Eurocode T-stub uses a “circular” effective length ℓeff = 2πm around a bolt. That length gives a collapse load of exactly 4π mp per bolt in mode 1 (section 9).

8. A load between two clamped edges: AISC’s own figure, derived

AISC 360-16 Commentary Fig. C-J10.10 shows a plate welded to a column web and pulled out of plane, with its yield lines. The same situation covers a stiffener or lug welded to any plate held by two stiff parallel edges. Span L between the clamped edges; the attached plate is c long and bp wide (its thickness plus weld legs).

Why the left pattern is wrong. Each trapezoid turns about a clamped edge, so its deflection grows with the distance from that edge. Along its sloping boundary the deflection is not zero, but the plate beyond is still. The work equation ignores that and gives P = 8 mp(c + 2e)/L, which goes to zero for a short load as e → 0. That is nonsense, and yl_work.m flags a deflection jump equal to δ.

Derivation of the right pattern (bp first taken as 0)

  1. Side trapezoids (2): turn about the clamped edges, θ = δ/(L/2) = 2δ/L. Each has a negative edge line c + 2e long and positive lines projecting to c + 2e. Work: 2 × mp(2δ/L) × 2(c + 2e) = 8 mpδ(c + 2e)/L.
  2. End triangles (2): turn about the transverse negative lines at distance e from the load ends, θ = δ/e. Each has a negative line L long and positive lines projecting to L. Work: 2 × mp(δ/e) × 2L = 4 mpδ L/e.
  3. WE = Pδ. So P(e) = mp [ 8c/L + 16e/L + 4L/e ].
  4. dP/de = 16/L − 4L/e² = 0 ⇒ e = L/2, and P = mp (8c/L + 16).

The 8c/L part is the strip of section 5 (clamped, b = c). The 16 mp is what the plate beyond the ends adds. With the attachment width bp kept and β = bp/L, η = c/L, the same steps give e = √(L(L − bp))/2 and

Pn = Fy t² [ 2η/(1 − β) + 4/√(1 − β) ]

(using 4mp = Fyt²). This is exactly AISC 360-05 Eq. K2-13, the chord-wall plastification of a rectangular HSS (in your AISC 13th ed. Manual, p. 16.1-132; also used in DG 24 Example 3.2 with its Fig. 3-4). Its Commentary calls it “an analytical yield line solution for flexure of the connecting chord face”. You have just derived a code equation by hand.

In AISC 360-16 the rectangular-HSS T-connection equations were removed from Chapter K; J10.10 now points to yield-line theory for transverse forces on plates in general.

HEB 240 web, tw = 10 mm, A36 (mp = 6 200 N); clear depth between root radii L = 240 − 2(17 + 21) = 164 mm; attached plate c = 120 mm, bp = 10 + 2 × 6 = 22 mm
η = 120/164 = 0.732;   β = 22/164 = 0.134;   1 − β = 0.866;   √0.866 = 0.931
Pn = 248 × 10² × [2(0.732)/0.866 + 4/0.931] = 24 800 × (1.690 + 4.298) = 24 800 × 5.989 = 148.5 kN;   φ = 0.9: 133.7 kN (e = 76.3 mm)

Chart 5. The same HEB 240 example: every value of e is an upper bound; the minimum at e = √(L(L − bp))/2 = 76.3 mm is the answer of the pattern.

Before using it on a real web. A column web also carries axial stress, which leaves less of Fy for bending; the HSS equations reduce the strength with Qf for that reason. The edges must really be clamped (thick flanges). And the 2005 Commentary says the equation “serves to limit connection deformations and is known to be well below the ultimate connection strength”, which is why it used φ = 1.00 there.

9. T-stubs and prying: bolted plates and anchor rods

A plate bolted down on both sides of a web that pulls it away (a T-stub flange, an end plate, a base plate in tension) folds along lines parallel to the web. Over an effective length ℓeff of the T-stub, each hinge line carries Mp = mp ℓeff. Three mechanisms compete (Bruneau eqs. 4.55–4.56; DG 16 §2.3; EN 1993-1-8 §6.2.4):

Each mode is a mechanism, so each gives an upper bound; the T-stub capacity is the smallest. Derive them by virtual work, with the web moving down by δ:

  1. Mode 1 (plate fails, bolts stay put): each half turns θ = δ/m, hinges at the web and at the bolt line. WI = 2 halves × 2 hinges × Mpθ = 4Mpδ/m. WE = Fδ ⇒ F1 = 4Mp/m. The plate tips press on the support: prying Q = Mp/n per bolt.
  2. Mode 2 (hinge at the web, bolts stretch at strength B): each half turns rigidly about its tip by θ = δ/(m + n), so each bolt stretches θn. WI = 2Mpθ + ΣB × θn ⇒ F2 = (2Mp + n ΣB)/(m + n).
  3. Mode 3 (plate rigid, bolts stretch): F3 = ΣB.
Example: m = 35, n = 30 (≤ 1.25 × 35 = 43.8), ℓeff = 150 mm, t = 12 A36; two Ø16 rods, Fu = 400 MPa
rod: φrn = 0.75 × (0.75 × 400) × 201 = 45.2 kN (AISC J3.6, Table J3.2: Fnt = 0.75Fu);   ΣB = 90.5 kN
φMp = 0.9 × 8 928 × 150 = 1.205 kN·m
F1 = 4 × 1.205×106/35 = 137.7 kN;   F2 = (2 × 1.205×106 + 30 × 90 480)/65 = 78.8 kN;   F3 = 90.5 kN   ⇒ mode 2 governs
prying per rod: Q = B − F2/2 = 45.2 − 39.4 = 5.8 kN

How AISC writes the same thing

AISC Manual Part 9 (as presented in Segui §7.8, eqs. 7.9–7.20) uses the same two-hinge model with a ratio α = (moment at the bolt line)/(moment at the stem). Setting α = 0 (no prying) in Segui eq. 7.18 gives the plate thickness at which the bolts reach full strength:

t = √( 4 B b′ / (φ p Fu) ),   b′ = b − d/2 Segui eq. 7.18 with α = 0

This is the mode 2/3 boundary above (Mp = mB per bolt), rewritten with two calibrations from tests: the bolt force is moved d/2 towards the stem (b′ instead of m), and Fu replaces Fy because tests showed the Fy model too conservative (Segui p. 423, citing Kulak, Fisher & Struik; Thornton 1992). Same mechanism, different calibration:

AISC: √(4 × 45 240 × 27 / (0.9 × 150 × 400)) = 9.5 mm
plastic theory with m and Fy: √(4 × 35 × 45 240 / (0.9 × 248 × 150)) = 13.8 mm

Use the method of the code you design to, and don’t mix the calibrations. For anchor rods pulling a base plate, AISC DG 1 also uses yield-line ideas (its λn′ = λ√(d bf)/4 is a “yield-line theory cantilever distance”, DG 1 §3.1.2).

Effective length. ℓeff is where the yield-line patterns come in: straight lines across the whole T-stub length, or local patterns around each bolt. EN 1993-1-8 Table 6.4 gives, for a bolt row away from the ends, ℓeff = min(2πm circular, 4m + 1.25e non-circular), with e the edge distance across the T-stub. The circular one is the fan of section 7: mode 1 per bolt = 2Mp/m = 2 × mp × 2πm / m = 4π mp.

10. End plates: code formulas built on yield lines

AISC Design Guide 16 and AISC 358 give each end-plate configuration as a yield-line pattern summed up in one parameter Y (mm). The plate strength is then

φMpl = φb Fpy tp² Y,   φb = 0.90 DG 16 Table 3-2

Because 4mp = Fyt², the parameter is just Y = WI/(4mpθ) for a rotation θ of the beam end about its compression flange.

Y = (bp/2) [ h1(1/pf + 1/s) − 1/2 ] + (2/g) [ h1(pf + s) ],   s = ½√(bp g); use pf = s if pf > s DG 16 Table 3-2

Doing the work sum yourself. Build the pattern of Figure 10 region by region: the region between the flange and the bolt line turns about the bolt line; so does the region between the bolt line and the s-line; the triangle at the web turns about the line from the compression flange through the bolt. Summing mpθℓ over all twelve lines gives exactly the two main terms, bph1(1/pf + 1/s)/2 + 2h1(pf + s)/g (checked by flushmech.m). The published Y has one more term, −bp/4. It lowers Y by about 3 % here, which is on the safe side; use the published formula.

IPE 240 (h = 240, tf = 9.8), bp = 140, g = 70, pf = 35:
h1 = 240 − 9.8 − 35 − 4.9 = 190.3;   s = ½√(140 × 70) = 49.5 (pf < s, OK)
Y = 70 [190.3 (1/35 + 1/49.5) − 0.5] + (2/70)[190.3 (35 + 49.5)] = 70 × 8.782 + 459.4 = 1 074 mm
tp = 12, A36: φMpl = 0.9 × 248 × 144 × 1 074 = 34.5 kN·m   (10 mm: 24.0; 16 mm: 61.4)

For comparison, the V2 edge moment is Mu = 19.05 kN·m. This is the kind of check the bolted end plate of cantilever_anchor_double_plate will need, with the through-rods as the bolts; the bolt and prying checks of DG 16 come with it.

Extended end plate, four bolts (4E). AISC 358-16 Table 6.2 (p. 9.2-31), with the required thickness tp,req = 1.11Mf/(φdFypYp), Eq. 6.8-5:

Yp = (bp/2) [ h1(1/pfi + 1/s) + h0(1/pfo) − 1/2 ] + (2/g) [ h1(pfi + s) ],   s = ½√(bp g); use pfi = s if pfi > s

Bruneau §4.6.2 (Fig. 4.31, eqs. 4.61–4.63) derives it step by step, including the optimum s = ½√(bfg) from ∂W/∂s = 0. That is the same minimisation you did for x in section 6.2.

Why the strips are safe. A strip with its own supports is a moment field that is in equilibrium and never exceeds mp, with zero moment everywhere else in the plate. The whole plate admits that same field, so by the lower-bound theorem it carries at least as much. The condition is that the supports the strip leans on really exist and are strong enough: the platinas and their fillets (report steps 4 and 5e check them).

When would V2 need a real yield-line check? Only if a strip check failed and you wanted to find the reserve. Then the mechanism would have to respect things the simple patterns of this page don’t have:

  • The plate is cast against concrete, so it can only move away from the concrete. The concrete is a one-sided support.
  • The flange, web and platinas are welded to the plate and are very stiff in their own planes. A yield line cannot cross them without also bending them.
  • The plate continues past every strip, so the end conditions are between pinned and clamped.

With the largest plate ratio at 0.67 on lower bounds, V2 does not need that. The report’s choice is the right one.

12. Pitfalls checklist

  1. Wrong mechanism = unsafe answer. Try several patterns, optimise every free dimension, take the smallest. Prefer code patterns confirmed by tests (DG 16, AISC 358, AISC 360 Chapter K, DG 24).
  2. Compatibility (rule 4). A region may meet the still plate only along its own axis. Check every boundary.
  3. Edges. Negative lines along clamped or continuous edges; none along simply supported or free edges. A “clamped” edge must really be stiff.
  4. Holes. A yield line through bolt holes is shorter: use the net length (Segui uses δ = 1 − d′/p at the bolt line). DG 16 ignores holes because its patterns were checked against tests.
  5. Shear and punching are not in the yield lines. Check J4.2: φVn = 1.0 × 0.6Fyt × length. For small patches shear can govern (section 7).
  6. What holds the plate must be stronger than the plate. Welds and bolts along the supports must carry the forces at collapse, prying included. V2 checks this as its strength order (report step 5e).
  7. Stiffness. A yield-line strength says nothing about how much the connection rotates in service. For moment connections, AISC 360-16 B3.4b (Commentary J10.10). DG 16 §2.4 adds a stiffness criterion for end plates.
  8. Hidden reserve, don’t count on it. Tests are often well above yield-line predictions (membrane action, strain hardening), but only at large deformations. That is why AISC calls K2-13 a deformation limit.
  9. Other stresses in the plate. Axial or in-plane stress lowers the mp left for bending (Qf in the HSS formulas).
  10. Fy or Fu? Plastic theory uses Fy. AISC prying uses Fu by calibration (section 9). Follow the code you design to.
  11. Units. mp in N (N·mm/mm). Pressure in MPa = N/mm². Keep mm throughout.
